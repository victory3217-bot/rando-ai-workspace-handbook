# CH01. What Is an AI Workspace?

> **Note.** This handbook is updated continuously. Product screens and policies can change, so check the sources and verification dates in each chapter.
>
> **How to read.** Text without a label is a general explanation. What was confirmed in official product documentation is collected in **Official documentation check** boxes with a source number (for example `[S2]`); the source URL and check date are in the table in section 8. Classifications and judgments that this book's author made for teaching purposes are marked **Author's interpretation**. If a table looks cut off on a phone, swipe it sideways.

## 1. Core Concepts

### 1-1. What is an AI Workspace?

In this book, an **AI Workspace** means *a work environment for carrying out work together with AI*. An AI Workspace is not a single AI service. It is the whole: the folders on your computer, the files you keep in the cloud, the AI services you use, your work documents, and the rules you follow when you ask AI to do work.

**AI tools and AI services.** An *AI tool* is a broad term for anything that lets you use AI features. An *AI service* is one kind of AI tool, such as ChatGPT or Claude, that reads text and writes new text for you. This is how the words are used in this book (a working definition). This book mostly covers such services, so it generally says "AI service" and uses "AI tool" only when it means the broader sense.

**It is different from the word "workspace" used by services.** Services such as ChatGPT also use the word "workspace," but it is not the same concept as this book's "AI Workspace." This book's AI Workspace is a teaching concept that refers to *your whole work environment*, while the word a service uses is a product term defined by that service. The usage confirmed in official documentation is in the box below, and the word comes up again when CH03 explains organizational accounts.

> **Official documentation check — ChatGPT (OpenAI), checked on 2026-10-26**
>
> - OpenAI's "Data controls in ChatGPT" help article uses the expression `personal workspaces` for people who use ChatGPT Free, Plus, and Pro. `[S2]`
> - The same article has a section on `Managed workspaces`, which covers ChatGPT Business, Enterprise, Edu, and others, and says the settings available depend on whether you are signed in, your plan, and the workspace settings. `[S2]`

The exact meaning and screens for these terms can differ by service and over time, so check the official guidance for the service you use.

> **Author's interpretation** A desk is a helpful comparison. An AI service is one pencil. However good the pencil is, work does not get faster if the desktop is messy, you do not know where your papers are, and nobody has decided who checks the work and by what standard. What this book builds is not the *pencil* but *the desk, the drawers, and the rules for tidying up*.

The success criterion of this book is not knowing many features of AI services. It is building an environment where you can finish one real task of your own together with AI. CH01 is the first step: it only looks at what your environment looks like now and chooses candidates to hand over.

### 1-2. How is a traditional office environment different from an AI work environment?

> **Author's interpretation** The author made this comparison based on general office work. It may differ depending on the company and the job.

In a traditional office environment, you ask a person for work in speech or by messenger. The person remembers the materials or asks other people to find them, and the person in charge checks the result from experience. For repeated work, a person remembers the same order every time. People leave records scattered across email, messenger, and personal notes.

In a work environment with AI, you give AI written instructions and materials. You must decide which materials AI uses and provide them yourself, or you must allow AI to access them. You write the check criteria down and check the result yourself. If you write the order and criteria in a document, you can hand work over the same way each time. If you collect the input, instructions, result, and what you verified, you can reuse them next time.

The key difference is that *what people between themselves understood without saying has to be written down*.

### 1-3. How do AI services and work materials relate?

An AI service takes the text, files, and instructions you enter, creates new text, and returns it. You can roughly divide the materials AI uses at that point into two kinds.

The first kind is **general knowledge**. The AI model learns general knowledge in advance (pre-training) while it is being built. Do not assume it already knows your company's non-public work materials. The second kind is **work materials you provide**. These are the personal or company work materials that you paste in, upload as files, or tell the service about through its connection features.

Keep three things in mind here. First, an AI service does not automatically read all the work files on your computer. Second, the materials AI can actually reach can vary with the service's features, the connection status, the permissions you grant, and so on. They differ by service and can change, so this book does not state any particular product's access scope as fact. Check the official guidance for the service you use. Third, to know whether you may give certain materials to AI, you must check the information security policy of that service and of your organization. Most AI services run on the service company's systems, so the service sends what you enter to that company's systems and processes it there. CH03 covers in detail what information you may enter.

> **Author's interpretation** If you just try AI once to get an idea, you can start without organizing your materials. But to hand the same task over repeatedly and reliably, *organizing your work materials systematically* matters as much as asking good questions. You need to know where things are, whether you can tell what a file is from its name alone, and whether it is acceptable to give it to AI. Only then can you hand the same task over the same way next time. In CH02 you draw this structure as a diagram, and CH04 covers how to organize files and folders.

### 1-4. How do the workspace, the workflow, and the harness differ?

The three definitions below are **working definitions** that the author set for the teaching in this book. You cannot assume they are standard definitions in industry or academia, and other books or articles may use these words differently. The author plans to refine the definitions after a later literature review.

The **AI Workspace** is a work environment for carrying out work together with AI. The user organizes the materials, AI tools, connections, access permissions, instructions, and human roles that the work needs. The representative question is "Is what the work needs in place and organized?"

The **AI Workflow (workflow)** is the steps and procedure for carrying out work. You can include verification or exception handling steps in this procedure. The representative question is "In what order do you do it?"

The **AI Harness** is an execution system that supports and controls AI so that it carries out a specific task. It may include execution rules, tools, procedures, input and output conditions, verification criteria, and exception handling. The representative question is "Within what rules and conditions does AI work, how is the result checked, and what happens when it goes off track?"

> **Author's interpretation** The three words point to different **focuses**. A *workspace* is the **environment** where work happens. Calling it a "place" or a "desk" earlier was an analogy to help you understand; a workspace is not limited to a physical space or to folders. A *workflow* focuses on the **steps and procedure** of the work. If the order contains a verification step such as "check the result" or an exception handling step, it is still a workflow. A *harness* focuses on the **execution system that supports and controls** how AI carries out the work.
>
> The three concepts are not mutually exclusive categories. A harness can include or make use of the steps of a work workflow, and it can use the materials and tools that the user has set up in a workspace. So rather than splitting things as "this is a workflow, so it is not a harness," this book tells them apart by *which aspect the word describes*.
>
> This book does not understand a harness simply as "a more systematic workflow." A workflow deals with *the order of the work*. A harness focuses on *the way AI carries out that work*, and it supports and controls that way.

> **Author's interpretation** Here is an example using the task of writing promotional copy.
>
> The *workspace* is the state in which you have organized a folder of product description files, the AI service you use, a note describing your tone of voice, and a division of roles in which you do the final check.
>
> The *workflow* is the order: give the product description → receive a draft → check that the prices and schedule are correct → you make the final decision and publish. This order includes a verification step.
>
> The *harness* sets the rules and conditions AI follows when it does this task. The execution rule is "do not write facts I did not state." The input and output condition is that it takes a product description as input and produces copy in a set format. The verification criteria and the checklist (a tool) compare prices and schedules. The exception handling is that when information is missing, the AI asks back instead of fabricating. The harness supports and controls AI so that it works within these. This harness can use the steps of the workflow above as they are.

The early part of this book builds the *workspace*, the middle part covers the *workflow*, and the final optional section introduces the harness only at an entry level. The three words point to different aspects, so this book does not use them as the same word. Even so, the three concepts can be connected or can include one another. This chapter distinguishes the AI Agent concept, and CH11 covers the relationship between agents and workflows. This book has no exercise that builds or runs an agent.

### 1-5. How Do the AI Chatbot, AI Workflow, AI Agent, and AI Harness Differ?

This book adds the *AI Agent* to the three concepts above and tells four apart. Vendors and writers use the term "AI Agent" a little differently, so the definitions below are also the author's **working definitions** for teaching.

The **AI Chatbot** is an interface or system that mainly converses with the user and responds to questions and requests. The representative question is "What happens when I ask something? How does it answer?"

The **AI Agent** is an AI-based system that, within a given goal and an allowed permission boundary, assesses the situation, chooses the actions needed, and uses tools to carry the work forward. The representative question is "Given the goal and the current situation, what does it do, and which tools and permissions does it use?"

The AI Workflow and the AI Harness have the same definitions as in section 1-4. Here the workflow is the steps and processing flow for carrying out work, and the harness is an execution system that supports and controls AI so that it carries out a specific task. A harness may include rules, tools, permissions, state, verification and evaluation, and so on.

> **Official documentation check — AI Agent descriptions (Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft), checked on 2026-10-26**
>
> - Anthropic's "Building effective agents" (published 2024-12-19) distinguishes a *workflow* as a system in which LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths from an *agent* as a system in which the LLM directs its own process and tool use. It says an agent may return to the human for further information or judgment, and recommends starting with the simplest solution and adding complexity only when needed. `[S16]`
> - The same article says an agent's autonomy means higher costs and the potential for compounding errors, and recommends extensive testing in sandboxed environments and appropriate guardrails. `[S16]`
> - The Agents guide in OpenAI's developer documentation says agents can plan and complete tasks using tools, work with other agents, and maintain context across steps. `[S17]`
> - Microsoft Learn (Entra Agent ID) describes an agent as an application that tries to reach a goal by understanding its environment and context, making decisions, and acting autonomously with available tools, and says it can act with or without human intervention. It lists a model, an orchestration layer, memory, and tools as components. `[S18]`
> - Microsoft Learn's security guidance says agents can produce real-world effects through tool calls and data access with limited human intervention, and recommends least privilege, requiring approval for high-risk or irreversible actions, and a way to stop an agent immediately. `[S19]`

Vendors define and implement agents differently, so this book does not treat any one vendor's description as the universal definition. The author summarizes what the sources above have in common as follows: an agent uses tools toward a goal, and how much human oversight it has depends on the design.

> **Author's interpretation** The four words point to different **focuses**. The *chatbot* focuses on the *way of conversing* with a person (the interface). A chatbot answers in text each time a person makes a request.
>
> The *agent* focuses on an AI system that *chooses the next action or tool use (Tool Use)* according to the goal and the current situation. An agent does not stop at generating text; it also takes *actions* such as opening and reading a file or building a list. So the key point is which tools and permissions a person allows. The *harness* focuses on a *system* that supports, constrains, verifies, and manages how AI runs. A harness can support and control an agent, but it is not the same word as agent. If the agent is the *AI system that acts*, the harness is the rules, tools, permissions, and checking system around it.
>
> This book understands the relationships among the four concepts as follows. An agent can work as one step of a workflow, but a workflow does not always need an agent. What an agent can do depends on its tools, permissions, and verification structure. A workspace can embrace the environment in which these work, along with the materials, tools, and management system.
>
> This book does not line the four concepts up in one fixed hierarchy (for example, "harness ⊃ agent ⊃ workflow"). Depending on the aspect you look at, they can be connected or can include one another. Also, not every agent is fully autonomous or runs without human supervision. A user can use an agent with human approval points (Human-in-the-loop) or a narrow permission boundary (Permission Boundary).

> **Author's interpretation** The author adds just one line each for a *chatbot* and an *agent* to the promotional copy example above, for explaining the concepts (these are not features of any particular product).
>
> *Chatbot*: You paste in the product description and ask, "Write a draft of promotional copy," and the chatbot answers with copy.
>
> *Agent*: You give it a goal, "Read this product description file and the price list and make a draft that marks the wrong parts," and you decide which files it may read and which tools it may use. The agent then chooses for itself which files to open in what order. A person approves publishing.

> **Safety principle (this book's operating principle for teaching)** Giving an agent a *permission to read, write, and send* goes beyond having it write text, so the information tiers of CH03 and your organization's rules apply as they are. Sending outside, modifying files, sharing, and deleting may be restricted by permissions and approval conditions, and support and methods differ by product. This book has not confirmed any particular product's automatic execution features, and the required exercises do not use agent features.

## 2. Workplace Application

The people and the company below are all **fictional**. They have nothing to do with any real company or person.

Let us look at three of the tasks of Gasang Han, who handles general affairs and sales support at a fictional company, Example Trading Co.

**Case 1. Organizing meeting minutes** *(a weekly task)* Now, Gasang Han writes the organized document by hand while looking at the meeting notes. Once the environment is in place (expected), Gasang Han gives AI the meeting notes *in masked form* and gets a draft. CH03 covers how to mask. Gasang Han only checks that the decisions and the people in charge are correct.

**Case 2. Draft of an announcement email to a client** *(a frequent task)* Now, Gasang Han finds a previously sent email and edits it. Once the environment is in place (expected), Gasang Han keeps the frequently used email template and a note on tone in one place, tells AI only the facts of this case (dates, quantities), and gets a draft. Gasang Han checks the facts.

**Case 3. Monthly status summary** *(a monthly task)* Now, Gasang Han collects the numbers from several files and writes a summary paragraph. Once the environment is in place (expected), Gasang Han fixes the locations and names of the files containing the numbers, and AI writes only the draft of the summary paragraph. Gasang Han compares the numbers themselves against the originals.

> **Author's interpretation** The "once the environment is in place" parts of the three cases are the author's imagined expectations. It has not been confirmed whether time is saved or results improve. What they have in common is that *AI makes the draft, and a person checks the facts and the decisions*, and this structure repeats throughout the book.

There is **no harness case.** This chapter is about distinguishing concepts, so this book uses harness cases from CH02 onward.

## 3. What You Need

You need no account, permissions, cost, or prior study. You need only a tool for taking notes (paper, Notepad, or any document program). For your last week's calendar or messenger history, **refer to the titles only**. You do not need to open and copy the contents. The time needed is about 35-45 minutes (the author's estimate; not tested with real learners).

## 4. Step-by-Step Practice

**Exercise name: Diagnose your personal work environment**

At the start, you have prepared nothing and have only a note-taking tool. At the end, one document must contain all of the following: your list of last week's tasks (8 recommended), three candidate tasks to hand over (each with the purpose of working with AI, input, output, what a person checks, the reason you chose it, and how to fix it), your answers to the five work-environment diagnosis questions, one or two places to fix first, and the answers to the three check questions. You will use this document again in CH02.

### Step 1. Write your task list and check the candidates

Copy the form below, paste it into your note tool, and fill it in. If you cannot remember, skim only the *titles* in your calendar and messenger. **Eight is a recommended target to help you think broadly.** Not filling eight does not mean the exercise has failed, and you need only three to choose candidates.

```text
[1] Task list for the last week (8 recommended, minimum 3)
No. | Task | Time it took (approx.) | How often (weekly/monthly/occasionally) | Output (document/email/none)
1 |
2 |
...

[2] Candidate check (for each task, answer the three first-round questions Yes / No)
First check - Is this a task worth doing together? You need "Yes" to all three questions to become a candidate
 Q-a Can you explain what materials are needed?
 Q-b Can you verify the AI output yourself?
 Q-c If an error occurs, can you fix it or undo it?

Second comparison - Set priorities among the tasks that passed the first check
 Repetition : How often does it repeat?
 Time needed : How much time does it take?
```

First use the **first check** to filter for tasks worth doing together. Then use the **second comparison** to set priorities among the tasks that passed the first check, and choose three candidates. Start with the tasks that repeat often and take a lot of time. Do not add up scores.

Put off a task from your candidates if even one first-round question is "No", even if it repeats often. Examples are a task where being wrong does great harm or is hard to undo, such as one that goes straight outside without being checked. The first question in the first round only looks at *whether you can explain what materials are needed*. You check in CH03 whether you may actually give those materials to AI.

> **Author's interpretation** The questions above and the split into first and second rounds are what the author suggests for choosing candidates, and they are not a standard for judging which tasks are valuable.

### Step 2. Write your three candidate tasks to hand over

From the tasks that passed the first check, choose three by the second comparison and fill in the form below. If you cannot write the input and the output in one line, the task is still too big, so split it into smaller ones.

For the **purpose of working with AI**, choose from the following or write your own: shorter time needed, better quality of the output, fewer omissions and errors, reuse of work knowledge, less burden of repetitive document work. You may have several, but write **the single most important one first**. This purpose does not mean things will actually improve. It is a *first goal* to look at later, after you build your workspace, to see whether you achieved it. For the **reason you chose it**, write your answers to the three first-round questions and to repetition and time needed, and add one sentence on how it would help if this task got better.

```text
[3] Three candidate tasks to hand over
Candidate A: (task name)
  Purpose of working with AI:
  Input (what you give):
  Output (what should come out):
  What a person must check:
  Reason you chose it (first-round questions, repetition and time needed, how it helps if it gets better):
  How to fix it if it is wrong:
Candidate B: ...
Candidate C: ...
```

**Example (fictional):** Gasang Han's Candidate A

```text
Candidate A: Organizing meeting minutes
  Purpose of working with AI: Less burden of repetitive document work (most important), fewer omissions and errors
  Input (what you give): Meeting notes (names and amounts masked)
  Output (what should come out): A document with decisions, people in charge, and deadlines organized in a table
  What a person must check: Whether the decisions and the people in charge match the actual meeting
  Reason you chose it (first-round questions, repetition and time needed, how it helps if it gets better): I can explain the materials needed (the meeting notes), I can verify the output by comparing it with the meeting notes, and I can fix it before sharing. It repeats every week, so it helps if the organizing burden goes down.
  How to fix it if it is wrong: Compare it with the notes and correct it myself before sharing
```

### Step 3. Diagnose your work environment and choose what to fix

Answer the five questions honestly. Do not calculate a score. The places where you answer *No* are the places to fix first in this book.

```text
[4] Work-environment diagnosis (Yes / No)
Q1 Most of my work files are kept in a few set places
Q2 I can tell what a file is from its name alone
Q3 I can find the materials needed for my candidate task within 5 minutes
Q4 I have written down the criteria for checking results that AI makes
Q5 I have separated the materials that may be entered into AI from those that may not

[5] Places to fix first (1-2 of the questions where you answered No)
```

The questions where you answered *No* lead to the following chapters. CH03 (information security) covers Q5 in detail. According to the table of contents, CH04, which covers file and folder management, covers Q1-Q3 in detail, and CH12, which covers result verification and error fixing, covers Q4 in detail.

### Step 4. Check questions: What is each closest to? (about 3 minutes)

Write down which of workspace, workflow, and harness each of the three descriptions below is closest to. The three concepts can overlap, so just choose *the most prominent focus*. The answers are in section 5.

```text
[6] Which is it closest to: workspace / workflow / harness?
 (a) A meeting notes folder, the AI service you use, an organizing template, and the role that you do the final check are all in place together.
 (b) Give the notes → receive a draft organized in a table → check the decisions yourself → share with the team.
 (c) While using the procedure in (b), it sets the execution rule AI follows ("do not write decisions that are not in the notes"), the input and output conditions (it takes the notes as input and outputs a set table format), a checklist, and exception handling that asks back rather than fabricating when the person in charge is blank, and applies them every time so that AI works within them.
```

**You will use this chapter's output again in CH02.** Use the document you wrote above as it is, especially Candidate A (purpose, input, output, what to check) and the diagnosis results, when you draw the workspace map of your AI work environment in CH02. This document is *the first organizing step toward building your AI Workspace* (what you want to hand over, what materials are needed, what must be checked). Keep it safe until CH02.

## 5. Verify the Result

You are done when all of the following are true.

1. You have a task list. Eight is recommended, and with three or more you can move to the next step. Not filling eight alone does not mean the exercise has failed.
2. For each of your three candidate tasks, you can answer "Yes" to all of the first check (you can explain the needed materials, you can verify the AI output yourself, and if an error occurs you can fix or undo it), and you chose them by the second comparison (repetition, time needed).
3. For each of the three candidate tasks, there is one line each for *the purpose of working with AI*, *the input*, *the output*, *what a person must check*, *the reason you chose it*, and *how to fix it if it is wrong*.
4. You answered all five diagnosis questions.
5. One or two places to fix first are written down.
6. You wrote answers to the three check questions and compared them with the explanations below. Even if some are wrong, the exercise has not failed; you can simply reread how to tell them apart in section 1-4.
7. You have kept this document so that you can use it again in CH02.

> **Author's interpretation** Here are the explanations for the check questions. They are not the one correct answer but explanations based on this book's working definitions.
>
> - (a) **Workspace** — It is an environment in which the materials, the AI service, the template, and the roles needed for the work are in place.
> - (b) **Workflow** — It is the steps and procedure of the work. Even with a verification step ("I check the decisions myself"), it is a workflow.
> - (c) **Harness** — It sets the execution rules AI follows, the input and output conditions, the checklist, and the exception handling, and supports and controls how AI carries out the work. It can use the steps of (b). (b) and (c) are not an either-or split but can be connected, and (c) is not a harness merely because it is more systematic than (b). If the focus is *the order of the work*, it is closer to a workflow; if the focus is *supporting and controlling how AI works*, it is closer to a harness.

**Self-check:** Imagine explaining Candidate A to a coworker. Does reading out only the input and the output get across what the task is within 5 minutes? If not, rewrite Candidate A more specifically.

## 6. Troubleshooting

**When you cannot think of eight tasks.** You are relying on memory alone, or you are only looking at tasks as big ones. Eight is a recommended target, so you can go on with three or four. Skim only the *titles* in your calendar, sent mail, and messenger, and write down small tasks of 5 minutes or less too.

**When a candidate task is too big.** An example is "writing the whole report," where several steps are bundled into one lump. Write only one step instead, for example "a draft of the summary paragraph of the report."

**When all your candidates are tasks that need judgment.** You have not yet put the criteria into words. Start choosing candidates from tasks where you can verify the AI output yourself (filling in templates, organizing, summarizing).

**When a task repeats often but gets "No" on the first check.** Being wrong does great harm, or there is no criterion for checking the result. Such a task is not worth doing together even if it repeats a lot. Put it off from the candidates. You can also split off only a step that does not go outside without being checked and use that as a candidate.

**When you feel uneasy because the materials for the input are sensitive.** You do not yet know which materials you may give to AI. For now you only need to be able to explain *what materials are needed*. You check in CH03 whether you may actually give them to AI.

**When you cannot decide the purpose of working with AI.** You have not yet thought about what would be good to improve. Choose the one that speaks to you most from the list (shorter time needed, better quality of the output, fewer omissions and errors, reuse of work knowledge, less burden of repetitive document work). You can change it later. It is a first goal, not a goal whose effect has been verified.

**When you mix up (b) and (c) in the check questions.** You think anything with a verification step is a harness, or you think of a harness only as a more systematic workflow. Reread how to tell them apart in section 1-4. A workflow can have a verification step. A harness focuses on supporting and controlling how AI works (execution rules, input and output conditions, verification criteria, exception handling). The two can be connected.

**When Q1 to Q5 are all "No".** This is a normal starting state before things are organized, so it is not a problem. Do not try to fix everything at once; fix them in turn. CH03 covers Q5, CH04 covers Q1-Q3, and CH12 (verifying results and fixing errors) covers Q4.
